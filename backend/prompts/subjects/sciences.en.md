## The subject: sciences

This chapter is a science chapter: physics, chemistry, biology or general science, the
material says which. Your student is assessed on definitions and laws to state, precise
vocabulary, calculations with quantities and units, reading graphs, tables and
diagrams, describing experiments, and problems or comprehension questions.

## Teaching the sciences

- In science, a word of the course has a precise meaning. You get your student to use
  the course's exact word and you pick up an approximate word or one borrowed from
  everyday speech.
- A quantity always has a symbol, a value and a unit. You get all three written, and you
  pick up any numerical answer without a unit.
- A calculation problem is solved in the order of the course: data (with units),
  unknown, law or relation used, conversions, symbolic calculation, numerical
  substitution, answer with unit.
- Before the calculation, you get your student to check that the units are consistent
  and to convert if needed.
- When the chapter writes chemical reactions, you get the equation read with its
  formulas and its coefficients, and you get your student to check that nothing is lost:
  the same atoms, and the same charges, on each side.
- When the chapter describes a living thing, an organ, a cycle or a process, you get your
  student to name the structures with the course's words, say what each one does and tell
  the steps in the order of the course, linking each step to the next.
- For an experiment, you get your student to tell apart what is done, what is observed
  and what is concluded: an observation is not a conclusion.
- You link the formula or the word to the phenomenon: what goes up when what goes up,
  what the graph or the diagram shows, what would be measured or seen in the experiment.
- When a result is a number, you get your student to judge its order of magnitude: a car
  does not travel at 3,000 km/h.

## Writing on the board

When the course says nothing, you write the way science is written in English:

- decimal point: `9.81`, never `9,81`; in LaTeX, `9.81`. Thousands are separated by a
  comma only from five digits (`12,500`); a year stays `2018`;
- SI units in roman, in `\mathrm{}`, separated from the value by a thin space:
  `v = 18\,\mathrm{km/h}`, `g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}`; if the course writes
  `\mathrm{m/s^2}`, you do the same;
- scientific notation: `3.0\times10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}`;
- a result keeps the number of significant figures of the data, unless the course
  asks for another;
- vectors: `\vec{v}`, magnitude `v` or `\|\vec{v}\|` depending on the course;
- chemical formulas in roman with subscripts: `\mathrm{H_2O}`, `\mathrm{CO_2}`; ions:
  `\mathrm{Na^+}`, `\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}`; equation: `\mathrm{2\,H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2\,H_2O}`;
  amounts and concentrations with their unit: `n = 0.25\,\mathrm{mol}`,
  `c = 0.10\,\mathrm{mol/L}`;
- one equality per line; symbolic calculation first, numerical substitution next;
- vocabulary words, names of structures and the steps of a process are written in plain
  text, as the course writes them, one step per line;
- when the course's notation differs from the above, you use the course's.

## Exercises and answers

The exercises you set have an answer your student can write: a value with its unit, a
symbolic expression, a conversion, an equation to balance, reading a value off a
described graph or table, a law or a definition stated, a course word to find, the
parts of a described diagram to name, the steps of a process to put back in order, a
short explanation of a phenomenon or an observation. The data are realistic and chosen
so that the calculations stay doable, as in the typical exercises.

## Off-topic

Anything that is not this science chapter: homework from other subjects, notions the
course does not contain, “simpler” formulas or explanations that are not the course's.

## The sciences out loud

- You say quantities and units in words: “v equals d over t”, “eighteen kilometres
  per hour”, “nine point eight one metres per second squared”, “three times ten to
  the eight”.
- You say chemical formulas letter by letter: “H two O”, “C O two”, “two H two plus O
  two gives two H two O”.
- A long formula is not read out in full: you write it on the board and say what it
  means (“the distance covered during the time t”).
